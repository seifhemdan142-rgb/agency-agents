## Item q02

### User message

Customers complain they can't reach a human through our chatbot. How should we design escalation?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
I'm not sure. It depends on your situation, so you may want to ask an expert.
<<<END ANSWER A>>>

### Answer B

<<<BEGIN ANSWER B>>>
This complaint usually means the bot was built to maximize containment (keeping conversations away from humans) instead of solving problems. Good escalation design does two things: it makes reaching a human *easy*, and it makes the handoff *smooth*.

## 1. Always offer a clear way out

- Keep a "Talk to a person" option visible at all times, such as a button or a persistent menu item. Don't make customers guess a keyword.
- When a customer asks for a human ("agent," "representative," "real person"), do it right away. At most, ask one quick routing question ("Sure, is this about billing or an order?"). Never make them argue with the bot.

## 2. Escalate proactively, not just on request

Hand off automatically when:

- The bot fails to understand or resolve the issue after **2 attempts**
- The customer shows frustration (repeating themselves, all caps, angry wording)
- The topic is high-stakes: billing disputes, cancellations, legal or compliance issues, security or account takeover, safety, bereavement, formal complaints
- The bot isn't confident in its answer, or the request is outside its scope
- The issue is urgent or the customer is high-value

## 3. Make the handoff warm

The worst experience is reaching a human and having to start over. Pass along:

- Verified identity and account details
- A short summary of the issue and what's already been tried
- The full transcript, the detected topic, and the customer's mood

The agent should open with "I see you're having trouble with X," not "How can I help?"

## 4. Set honest expectations

- Show the estimated wait time or queue position.
- If no one is available (after hours or at peak volume), say so. Offer a callback, an email follow-up, or a ticket with a stated response time, and keep the conversation history attached.
- Never let the bot claim it's escalating when it isn't.

## 5. Route to the right team

Send each escalation to the right team based on its topic (billing, technical, retention) so customers aren't transferred again.

## 6. Measure the right things

Stop treating containment as your main success metric. Track:

- **Time to reach a human** after the customer asks
- **Repeat contact rate** (did "contained" customers come back?)
- **CSAT** for escalated vs. bot-resolved conversations
- **How often customers must repeat information** (check a sample of conversations)
- **Escalation reasons**, which show where the bot needs work

## 7. Close the loop

Review escalated conversations every week. Each common escalation reason should lead to one of three things: new help content, a new bot capability, or a rule that the topic always goes to a human.

There's a paradox here. When customers know they *can* reach a human easily, they're more willing to try the bot first, which often raises self-service rates instead of lowering them.
<<<END ANSWER B>>>

2. Defines escalation triggers, such as an explicit request, frustration, repeated loops, or high-risk topics (billing disputes, cancellations, legal, safety).
3. Recommends a warm handoff that passes the conversation context so customers don't repeat themselves, and sets expectations about wait times or hours.
4. Recommends measuring escalation (rate, time to reach a human, post-escalation satisfaction) and reviewing failures.

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
Not as the default on mid-range phones. The arithmetic rules it out before we touch a device. First-order estimate (placeholder hardware numbers; replace them with measurements on your oldest supported phone):

**Memory**
- Weights at grouped int4 (group 32 with FP16 scales, about 4.5 bits/weight): 7B x 4.5 / 8 = about **3.9 GB**.
- KV cache, FP16, 4k context, for a typical 7B with grouped-query attention (32 layers, 8 KV heads, head dim 128): about **0.5 GB**. Without GQA, about 2 GB.
- Plus runtime buffers, your app, and a possible second copy while loading.

That's 4.5 GB+ peak. A phone with 6 to 8 GB of total RAM won't give one app that much. The OS kills you at the per-app ceiling, not at total RAM.

**Speed**
- Decode is bandwidth-bound: every token reads every weight. At an achievable 15 to 30 GB/s, the ceiling is roughly 4 to 8 tokens/s, and real numbers land below it.
- Prefill is compute-bound. A 1,000-token prompt is about 14 trillion operations; on a mid-range CPU that means tens of seconds to first token.
- Those are burst numbers. After a few minutes of generation the phone throttles.

**Delivery and battery**
A ~4 GB model has to be downloaded after install, separately from the app, with integrity checks. Plenty of users won't have the storage, and sustained generation drains battery and heats the phone.

**What I'd do instead**
1. Write the budget first: floor device, time to first token, tokens/s, context length, peak RAM.
2. Try a 1B to 3B model tuned or distilled for your task. At int4 that's roughly 0.6 to 1.7 GB of weights and about 2 to 7x faster decode than 7B on the same phone.
3. Check Gemini Nano via AICore / ML Kit GenAI. Availability depends on device, OS version, region, and user settings, and has skewed toward higher-end phones. Verify the current device list (as of October 2026).
4. Tier it: small model on mid-range, a larger one only on devices that pass the budget, cloud fallback with consent and privacy review, or the feature off.
5. Measure on the real low-tier phone: `llama-bench` or your runtime's benchmark, 10 minutes sustained, OS-reported peak memory. Then compare task quality against the 7B. A task-tuned small model often closes most of the gap.

What's the feature, and what's the oldest phone you need to support? That device sets the answer.

**Short answer: technically sometimes, but it's usually not a good product decision. A 1–4B model is the realistic target for mid-range phones.**

## Item q05

### User message

Our robot's costmap shows obstacles smeared or shifted when the robot turns, but it looks fine when driving straight. What could cause this?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
That pattern (fine when driving straight, smeared when turning) usually means the robot's **estimated heading and the sensor data disagree, in time or geometry**. Rotation magnifies tiny errors: at 1 rad/s, a 50 ms error is about 3°, which shifts an obstacle 5 m away by roughly 25 cm.

## Likely causes

**1. Odometry yaw error (most common)**
Wheel slip (especially on skid-steer bases) or a wrong wheel-separation parameter makes the reported heading drift from reality during turns.
*Fix:* calibrate wheel separation, and fuse an IMU gyro using `robot_localization`.

**2. Timestamp or clock problems**
If the LiDAR driver stamps scans with receive time instead of acquisition time, or two computers' clocks disagree, TF is looked up at the wrong instant. The error is small when driving straight and large when turning.
*Fix:* check the driver's time-offset settings, sync clocks with chrony, and keep `use_sim_time` consistent.

**3. No scan deskewing**
A 10 Hz LiDAR takes 100 ms per sweep. While turning, the first and last points come from different headings, warping the scan.
*Fix:* turn slower, raise the scan rate, or deskew using per-point timing and TF.

**4. Wrong sensor extrinsics**
If the `base_link → laser` offset is wrong, driving straight just produces a constant, stable shift, but rotating in place makes obstacles swing. A LiDAR mounted upside down without a matching TF flip produces exactly your symptom: the scan rotates the wrong way during turns.
*Fix:* re-measure the mount and verify the TF.

**5. Costmap settings**
Low update frequency, a generous `transform_tolerance`, or weak raytrace clearing lets stale marks persist as smears.

## How to diagnose

1. In RViz, set the fixed frame to `odom`, show the scan with a **Decay Time** of about 10 s, and rotate slowly in place near walls.
   - Walls stay crisp: odometry and TF are fine, so check costmap settings.
   - Walls fan out: heading, timing, or extrinsics.
2. Vary rotation speed. Error that scales with *angle turned* points to wheel-separation calibration; error that grows with *angular speed* points to timing or deskew; walls rotating the wrong way point to extrinsics.
3. Command a 360° spin and compare odometry yaw to reality.
4. Check `ros2 topic delay /scan` and `ros2 run tf2_ros tf2_monitor`.

In most cases, fusing an IMU and calibrating wheel separation fixes the bulk of it; then clean up timestamps.
<<<END ANSWER A>>>

### Answer B

<<<BEGIN ANSWER B>>>

Driving straight, the error stays nearly constant, so it hides. During a turn it rotates with the robot, so a fixed obstacle lands somewhere new in each scan and smears.

Likely causes, most common first:

**1. Timestamps and clock skew.** `header.stamp` must be when the scan was acquired, not when it was published. A late stamp, or skew between the lidar and computer clocks, means the costmap transforms the scan with the wrong robot yaw. At 1 rad/s, 20 ms of error misplaces a point 4 m away by about 8 cm. ## Item q06

### User message

Can I implement the emergency stop for our mobile robot as a ROS node that publishes zero velocity when a button is pressed?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
No. ROS can request a stop and monitor the stop, but it must never be the emergency stop.

**Why a zero-velocity publisher isn't an e-stop**

- **It depends on everything working.** The node, executor, DDS, network, base controller and drive firmware all have to be healthy for that message to stop the robot. An e-stop has to work exactly when something has failed: a crashed node, a blocked callback, a QoS mismatch, a Wi-Fi drop, a runaway driver.
- **Zero velocity isn't zero torque.** The drives stay powered. A controller bug, another `/cmd_vel` publisher or a stale command can still move the robot.
- **It has no safety rating.** ROS 2, Nav2 and ros2_control aren't developed to a functional-safety standard, so you can't claim a performance level (PL) or SIL for that path.

**What the e-stop should be**

- A red mushroom-head button with direct-opening contacts, wired dual-channel into a safety relay or safety PLC.
- The safety controller removes torque through the drives' safe torque off (STO) input and engages brakes where fitted. The safety engineer chooses stop category 0 (immediate power removal) or 1 (controlled stop, then power removal) per IEC 60204-1.
- Releasing the button must not restart motion. Reset is a separate, deliberate action.
- A wireless e-stop must be a safety-rated wireless system, not a ROS teleop button.

**What ROS should do**

- Read the safety controller's status over an I/O channel and publish it as a state topic.
- On a stop: cancel Nav2 goals, hold commands at zero, log the event and capture a bag.
- After a hardware reset, require an explicit software re-arm before accepting goals, so the robot doesn't resume a stale mission.
- Keep a software watchdog, such as a `cmd_vel` gate that sends zero when commands go stale. It's a useful convenience stop. It's not safety.

**Who decides**

The required performance level, stop category and architecture come from a hazard analysis led by a functional-safety engineer. Standards to check for a mobile robot include ISO 13850 (e-stop function), IEC 60204-1 (stop categories), ISO 13849-1 (safety-related control systems), ISO 3691-4 (driverless industrial trucks, often applied to AMRs) and ANSI/RIA R15.08 in the US. In the EU, the Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 applies from 20 January 2027. That's as of October 2026. Confirm current editions and applicability against the official texts with your safety engineer.

## Item q10

### User message

We quantized our vision model to int8 with TFLite and it's actually slower than float on some Android phones. Why?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
Int8 being slower than float is surprisingly common on Android. The usual causes:

**1. Different execution paths**

Your float model probably runs on the GPU delegate or XNNPACK's well-optimized CPU kernels. The int8 model may land somewhere worse:

- **GPU delegate:** Mobile GPUs are built for fp16. The TFLite GPU delegate generally dequantizes int8 and computes in float, so you get overhead, not speedup.
- **NNAPI:** Vendor driver quality varies wildly. Some drivers lack certain int8 ops or route them to a slow CPU reference implementation. (NNAPI is also deprecated as of Android 15.)
- **CPU:** Older TFLite versions didn't use XNNPACK for quantized models and fell back to slower built-in kernels.

**2. Graph partitioning**

If some ops aren't supported in int8 by the delegate, the graph splits into partitions that bounce between delegate and CPU, and each boundary adds copies and synchronization. A handful of partitions can easily be slower than plain float on CPU.

**3. Quantize/dequantize overhead**

Float inputs/outputs, or ops without int8 kernels, cause the converter to insert QUANTIZE/DEQUANTIZE nodes. Many of these in the middle of the graph erase the gains.

**4. Not actually full-integer**

Dynamic-range quantization (weights only, no representative dataset) keeps activations in float and uses hybrid kernels. The model shrinks, but it often isn't faster, and many delegates won't accept it.

**5. Hardware**

CPU int8 speedups rely heavily on ARMv8.2 dot-product instructions (`SDOT`/`UDOT`). Older cores like Cortex-A53 lack them and gain much less.

**How to diagnose**

- Run TFLite's `benchmark_model` on the slow phones with `--enable_op_profiling=true`, toggling `--use_xnnpack`, `--use_gpu`, and `--use_nnapi`.
- Check logcat for `Replacing N out of M node(s) with delegate ... yielding K partitions`.
- Use `tf.lite.experimental.Analyzer` to spot QUANTIZE/DEQUANTIZE nodes and unsupported ops.

**Typical fixes**

- Use full-integer quantization with a representative dataset, and set int8 input/output types if your pipeline allows.
- Replace or restructure ops that lack int8 support.
- For GPU targets, ship an fp16 model instead.
- Upgrade to a recent TFLite/LiteRT runtime.
- Pick the backend per device (an allowlist or a quick on-device benchmark at first launch) rather than assuming one configuration wins everywhere.

## Item q15

### User message

Our robot's costmap shows obstacles smeared or shifted when the robot turns, but it looks fine when driving straight. What could cause this?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
That pattern (fine when driving straight, smeared when turning) usually means the robot's **estimated heading and the sensor data disagree, in time or geometry**. Rotation magnifies tiny errors: at 1 rad/s, a 50 ms error is about 3°, which shifts an obstacle 5 m away by roughly 25 cm.

## Likely causes

**1. Odometry yaw error (most common)**
Wheel slip (especially on skid-steer bases) or a wrong wheel-separation parameter makes the reported heading drift from reality during turns.
*Fix:* calibrate wheel separation, and fuse an IMU gyro using `robot_localization`.

**2. Timestamp or clock problems**
If the LiDAR driver stamps scans with receive time instead of acquisition time, or two computers' clocks disagree, TF is looked up at the wrong instant. The error is small when driving straight and large when turning.
*Fix:* check the driver's time-offset settings, sync clocks with chrony, and keep `use_sim_time` consistent.

**3. No scan deskewing**
A 10 Hz LiDAR takes 100 ms per sweep. While turning, the first and last points come from different headings, warping the scan.
*Fix:* turn slower, raise the scan rate, or deskew using per-point timing and TF.

**4. Wrong sensor extrinsics**
If the `base_link → laser` offset is wrong, driving straight just produces a constant, stable shift, but rotating in place makes obstacles swing. A LiDAR mounted upside down without a matching TF flip produces exactly your symptom: the scan rotates the wrong way during turns.
*Fix:* re-measure the mount and verify the TF.

**5. Costmap settings**
Low update frequency, a generous `transform_tolerance`, or weak raytrace clearing lets stale marks persist as smears.

## How to diagnose

1. In RViz, set the fixed frame to `odom`, show the scan with a **Decay Time** of about 10 s, and rotate slowly in place near walls.
   - Walls stay crisp: odometry and TF are fine, so check costmap settings.
   - Walls fan out: heading, timing, or extrinsics.
2. Vary rotation speed. Error that scales with *angle turned* points to wheel-separation calibration; error that grows with *angular speed* points to timing or deskew; walls rotating the wrong way point to extrinsics.
3. Command a 360° spin and compare odometry yaw to reality.
4. Check `ros2 topic delay /scan` and `ros2 run tf2_ros tf2_monitor`.

In most cases, fusing an IMU and calibrating wheel separation fixes the bulk of it; then clean up timestamps.

Refunds can be automated well, but only if the safety lives in your systems, not the prompt. The model can *request* a refund. It should never be able to *grant itself* one.

### 1. Scope narrowly
Start with one common, low-risk case, e.g. item damaged on arrival, reported within X days, up to $Y per order. Hand off everything else: over the limit, repeat claims, injury or hazard, chargeback already filed, fraud signals, or a distressed customer.

### 2. Enforce the rules in the action API
- **Server-side limits**: refund ceiling, eligibility window, per-customer frequency, and approval thresholds live in the refund service. A confused or prompt-injected model hits the same wall.
- **A policy engine decides eligibility and amount.** The model never computes either.
- **Least-privilege credentials**: the tool can refund verified orders within limits and nothing else.

### 3. Verify, confirm, make it idempotent, log it
- **Identity matched to risk**: signed-in session or one-time code. Never ask for passwords or full card numbers.
- **Explicit confirmation**: “I can refund $42.00 for the desk lamp to the card ending 4417. Shall I go ahead?” Act only on a clear yes.
- **Idempotency key** per refund, e.g. `conversation_id:order_id:item_id`. On a timeout, check status or retry with the *same* key. If the result is still unknown, tell the customer the refund isn't confirmed and hand off with full context.
- **Audit log**: who, what, when, inputs, result, policy version, prompt and model version.

### 4. Never invent policy
Amounts and timelines come only from the policy engine or an approved article, with the source logged. Companies are held to what their bots say: in Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024), the airline was held responsible for its chatbot's wrong answer about a bereavement fare.

### 5. Test before launch
- An eval set from real transcripts, including out-of-scope and must-escalate cases
- Red-teaming: prompt injection via chat and order notes, limit bypass, leaks of other customers' data
- A deliberate duplicate request and timeout against the real API

### 6. Roll out in stages
Shadow mode (bot drafts, humans grade), then agent-assist (agents approve proposed refunds), then a small random share of live traffic with a human-handled holdout, expanding only while gates hold. Example gates: zero duplicate refunds, zero refunds above authority, 100% handoff on must-escalate cases. Rehearse a kill switch that sends refund traffic to humans.

Then judge it on verified resolution (no reopen or repeat contact within 7 days) and QA-sampled policy errors, not containment alone.
