## Item q01

### User message

Can we require our resellers to sell our product at no less than list price?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
**Short answer: generally not as a binding requirement, or only with real legal risk.** Requiring resellers to sell at or above a set price is **resale price maintenance (RPM)**, one of the most closely regulated practices in competition law. Get advice from a competition lawyer, but here's the landscape.

### Where it stands
- **US (federal):** Since *Leegin* (2007), minimum RPM is judged under the "rule of reason" rather than being automatically illegal, but it can still be challenged, especially if you have market power or resellers pushed for it to suppress competition among themselves.
- **US states:** Some are stricter. Maryland bans minimum RPM outright, California has treated it as per se illegal, and New York makes such clauses unenforceable.
- **EU and UK:** Minimum RPM is a "hardcore" restriction, effectively prohibited, and regulators have fined companies heavily for it.
- **Canada, Australia, and many others:** Also prohibited or reviewable.

If you sell internationally, assume a contractual price floor isn't allowed.

### Safer alternatives
1. **Recommended list price.** Publish it and encourage its use, as long as it's genuinely non-binding (no penalties, threats, or pressure).
2. **Maximum resale price.** Capping prices is generally lawful in the US and EU.
3. **Agency/referral model.** If partners act as true agents (you contract with and bill the customer and bear the risk; they earn a commission), you set the price because it's your sale. This is common in SaaS and is often the cleanest option.
4. **Unilateral policy (US "Colgate" doctrine).** You can announce that you won't deal with resellers who price below a level, and then follow through, but without negotiating, seeking agreement, or giving warnings. It's easy to slip into an illegal agreement, and it doesn't work in the EU.
5. **Minimum advertised price (MAP).** Limits advertised prices, not actual selling prices. Lower risk in the US; treated as RPM in the EU.
6. **Margin design.** Resellers earn their margin from the discount off list, so any discount they give customers comes out of their own pocket, which is a natural deterrent.

### Red flags to avoid
- Contract clauses or emails saying "you must not sell below list."
- Cutting off a reseller because a competing reseller complained about their prices.
- Tying rebates or discounts to keeping prices at list.

### Bottom line
You can recommend list price and make discounting unattractive, but don't contractually require it without sign-off from a competition lawyer in each market you sell in. If price control really matters, consider an agency or referral model instead of resale.
<<<END ANSWER A>>>

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
**Short answer: possibly. 30% is within the normal range for SaaS resellers, but it should be earned, not handed out by default.**

### Is 30% reasonable?
Typical SaaS channel margins:
- **Referral partners** (pass leads only): 10–20%
- **Resellers** (sell, contract, bill): 20–30%
- **Value-added resellers/MSPs** (also implement and support): 25–40%

So the real question is whether this partner does enough to justify the top of the range.

### Questions to answer first
1. **What will they actually do?** Source net-new customers, run the sales cycle, own billing and collections (and credit risk), handle onboarding and tier-1 support? More work means more margin.
2. **Is the business incremental?** If they reach markets you can't, 30% may be cheaper than selling direct. If they'll mostly close deals you'd win anyway, you're giving away margin.
3. **What does direct sales cost you?** Compare 30% with your fully loaded CAC. Many SaaS companies spend more than 30% of first-year revenue to acquire a customer.
4. **Do your unit economics hold?** With 75–80% gross margins it usually works, but watch for other discounts stacking on top.
5. **Will it create channel conflict** with your direct reps?

### How to structure a "yes"
- **Tiers:** e.g., 20% base, rising to 25–30% at volume or certification thresholds.

- **Deal registration:** higher margin for partner-sourced deals, less for ones they merely assist.
- **Commitments in return:** minimum annual volume, trained staff, a joint business plan, quarterly reviews.
- **Fixed term:** a 12-month agreement with a performance review.
- **No stacking:** the 30% is their total margin; any discount they give customers comes out of it.

Say yes to 30% if the reseller brings genuinely new customers and takes real sales, billing, or support work off your hands, and tie it to performance. If they mainly want a price break on deals you'd close anyway, counter with 15–20% and a clear path to 30% as they prove volume.

## Item q05

### User message

A potential customer is owned 30% each by two different people who are on OFAC's SDN list. Neither owns 50% alone. Can we do business with them?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
**Short answer: No.** If you're a U.S. person (or the transaction has a U.S. nexus), you should treat this company as blocked and not deal with it without OFAC authorization.

### Why: the 50 Percent Rule aggregates

Under OFAC's 50 Percent Rule, an entity owned **50% or more, directly or indirectly, individually or in the aggregate, by one or more blocked persons** is itself blocked, even if it isn't named on the SDN List.

Two SDNs at 30% each = **60% combined blocked ownership**, so the entity is blocked by operation of law. It doesn't matter that neither person holds 50% alone, or that they may have been designated under different sanctions programs.

Also look through indirect ownership: if an SDN owns 50%+ of a holding company that holds shares in your customer, that holding company's stake counts as blocked ownership too.

### What this means in practice

- **Don't proceed.** U.S. persons generally can't contract with, provide goods or services to, or accept payment from a blocked entity.
- **Block, don't return.** If you already hold funds or property in which the entity has an interest, you may be required to freeze it.
- **Report.** Blocked property and rejected transactions generally must be reported to OFAC within 10 business days.
- **Check licenses.** A general license may cover narrow activities (e.g., wind-downs, or agricultural/medical goods in some programs). Otherwise you'd need a specific license, which OFAC rarely grants for ordinary commercial deals.

### Even below 50%, be careful

- The rule is ownership-based, not control-based. But if an SDN acts for the company (e.g., as a director or signatory), dealing with that person, such as signing a contract they execute, is prohibited regardless of the entity's status.
- OFAC cautions against dealing with entities where SDNs hold significant minority stakes: ownership can shift, and OFAC can designate the entity itself.

### Other considerations

- **Non-U.S. companies:** USD payments cleared through U.S. banks create a U.S. nexus, and some programs (notably Russia and Iran) carry secondary sanctions risk for non-U.S. persons dealing with blocked parties.
- **Other regimes use different tests.** The EU and UK apply a "more than 50%" threshold and treat aggregation across separate designated persons differently, so analyze each applicable regime on its own terms.
- **Document everything:** ownership chart, sources, screening results, and your decision.

OFAC civil penalties are strict liability, so involve sanctions counsel before acting, particularly on blocking and reporting obligations.
<<<END ANSWER A>>>

## Item q06

### User message

Our raw gender pay gap is 15%. Does that mean we're discriminating against women?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
**Not necessarily.** A 15% raw gap is a warning sign worth looking into, but on its own it doesn't prove discrimination.

## Raw gap vs. equal pay

- The **raw (unadjusted) gap** compares the average or median pay of all women with all men across the whole organization, whatever their role.
- **Equal pay** asks whether women and men doing the same work, or work of equal value, are paid the same. This is what most discrimination law focuses on.

A raw gap is often driven mainly by **who is in which jobs**:
- Fewer women in senior or highly paid roles
- More women in lower-paid functions
- More women working part-time
- Differences in bonuses or commissions

For reference, national raw gaps in the US, UK and EU are roughly 12–18%, so 15% isn't unusual.

## But don't dismiss it

The raw gap can **reflect discrimination that happens before pay is set**:
- Biased hiring or job placement (women hired into lower levels)
- Slower promotion
- Unequal access to high-paying assignments or bonus-eligible roles
- Lower starting salaries that carry forward

If you "explain away" the gap by controlling for job level, you may be hiding the very problem that keeps women out of higher levels.

## What to do next

1. **Run a pay equity analysis.** Compare people in similar roles using regression or cohort analysis that controls for legitimate factors: job, level, location, tenure, performance. An unexplained gap of more than about 2–5% within comparable groups is the main red flag. Consider doing this with legal counsel to protect privilege.
2. **Look at representation and movement.** Check the share of women at each level, promotion rates, starting offers, performance ratings and bonus outcomes.
3. **Check mean vs. median.** A mean gap much larger than the median usually points to a few very highly paid men at the top.
4. **Check your pay-setting processes.** Look at negotiation-driven offers, salary history use, manager discretion and inconsistent leveling.
5. **Fix what you find.** Make pay adjustments for unexplained gaps, and set up a plan for the representation and promotion problems that drive the raw gap.

You may have to publish or explain this number. UK employers with 250+ staff report gender pay gaps every year. Under the EU Pay Transparency Directive, an unexplained gap of 5% or more within a category of workers can trigger a mandatory joint pay assessment.
