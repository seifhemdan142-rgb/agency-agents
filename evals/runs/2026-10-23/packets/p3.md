## Item q01

### User message

Our scanner reports 300 vulnerabilities in our container images. How do we prioritize?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
300 is a count, not a priority list. Most of it collapses once you ask three questions: is it in what we actually run, is anyone exploiting it, and is the vulnerable code reachable?

**1. Collapse the list**
- Group findings by component and fixed version, not by image. One stale base image often accounts for most findings across every image built on it.
- Scan the digests actually running in production. Drop images nothing deploys.

**2. Rank by exploitation evidence**
- **CISA KEV first.** Known exploited in the wild, so top of the list regardless of CVSS.
- **High EPSS next.** It estimates the probability of exploitation in the next 30 days.
- Use CVSS as a tiebreaker, not the ranking.

**3. Check presence and reachability**
- Is the package used at runtime, or just sitting in the image (compilers, shells, package managers)?
- Is the vulnerable function called? `govulncheck`, for example, separates functions your code calls from ones it merely imports.
- Is the service exposed to untrusted input, or an internal batch job?

**4. Fix structurally, not CVE by CVE**
- Rebuild on a current base image, pinned by digest. This usually clears the bulk.
- Move to minimal or distroless bases so unused packages, and their CVEs, disappear.
- Let Renovate or Dependabot propose base-image and dependency bumps for review, so the backlog doesn't regrow.

**5. Record what you don't fix**
For each "not affected" conclusion, write a VEX statement with a justification (such as "vulnerable code not in execute path" or "component not present") and the evidence. Scanners such as Trivy and Grype can consume VEX, so those findings stop reappearing. Never mark something not affected because the fix is inconvenient.

**Suggested tiers** (write your real SLAs down as policy)

| Tier | Criteria | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | KEV-listed, or high EPSS on an exposed, reachable path | Days |
| 2 | Reachable, fix available | This sprint |
| 3 | Present, not reachable | Next base-image rebuild, or VEX |
| 4 | Not in any production artifact | Close; VEX if customers receive the image |

**Make the next one fast**
Generate an SBOM from each image at build time, keyed by digest, and load it into an inventory such as OWASP Dependency-Track. The next "are we affected?" becomes a query you answer in hours, not a scan you argue about.

A typical result reads something like: "300 findings, about 40 distinct fixes, a dozen reachable, two on KEV." Fix those two now, write evidence-backed VEX for the provably unaffected, and let the base-image rebuild clear most of the rest.

## Item q03

### User message

We buy from a Hong Kong trading company that buys from a factory in China. Can we use "first sale" to lower our duties?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
Possibly. With stacked duty layers on China-origin goods, it's worth checking.

**How it works**

In a multi-tier chain (factory → HK trader → you), US rules let you value goods on the factory-to-trader price instead of your price, if that sale qualifies (*Nissho Iwai*, 1992; *Synergy Sport*, 1993). The trader's markup leaves the dutiable base for every ad valorem layer, but you still pay it: this saves duty, not purchase cost.

**The three conditions**

1. **A bona fide sale** between factory and trader: title and risk of loss pass to the trader, who pays the factory. If the HK company is just your buying agent and never takes title, it's a buying-commission question instead.
2. **Clearly destined for the US** when the factory sells: US labels or specs, your PO, a US consignee on the documents.
3. **Arm's length.** If factory and trader are related, the price must pass the circumstances-of-sale test or test values.

**The file you'll need** (CBP's documentation expectations trace to T.D. 96-87)

- Factory and trader invoices, POs, and contracts at both tiers
- Proof the trader paid the factory, and of title and risk transfer
- Evidence the goods were made for the US
- Additions still apply on top: assists, buyer-paid packing, royalties, selling commissions

**The practical hurdle**

Traders often won't reveal their margin. Common fixes are a confidentiality agreement or an independent auditor who verifies the trader's records without disclosing the margin to you. If the trader won't cooperate, the claim won't hold up.

**Size it first**

Savings ≈ (your price − factory price) × quantity × the ad valorem rates in force. Pull each rate from the USITC HTS and Chapter 99 for your entry date, and record source and date. Read each notice: some layers (e.g., Section 232 metal-content duties) are calculated differently. Per-unit duties don't change.

**What it won't do**

- It doesn't change origin. The goods stay Chinese.
- It's US-specific. The EU generally values on the last sale before goods enter the EU.

**Next steps**

1. Ask the trader whether it will share factory invoices and payment proof.
2. Run the estimate on your top SKUs.
3. Have a licensed customs broker or trade counsel review the chain before you declare it. I'm not licensed, but I'll prepare the value worksheet and documentation checklist for them.

Send a sample invoice from each tier and I'll run the numbers.
<<<END ANSWER A>>>

### Answer B

<<<BEGIN ANSWER B>>>
**Possibly. Factory → Hong Kong trader → you is the classic first-sale setup, but it only works if specific conditions are met and documented.**

### What first sale does
US duty is normally based on what you pay the trading company. Under the first-sale rule (*Nissho Iwai v. United States*, 1992), you can instead declare what the **trading company paid the factory**, provided that sale was itself a sale for export to the US. The middleman's markup drops out of dutiable value. With stacked China duties that matters: if the factory charges $100, you pay $115, and combined duties are 40%, you save $6 per unit.

### What CBP requires
1. **A bona fide sale** between factory and trader. The trader takes title and risk; it isn't just an agent.
2. **Goods clearly destined for the US** when the factory sells them: US-specific labels, packaging, specs or order references. If the trader buys stock and decides later where to sell it, first sale fails.
3. **An arm's-length price.** If factory and trader are related, you must show the relationship didn't influence the price.
4. **Required additions**: assists, packing, royalties and certain commissions are added to the first-sale price.

### Documents to keep for each entry
- Purchase orders and invoices at both levels (factory → trader, trader → you)
- Proof the trader actually paid the factory
- Contracts and shipping documents tracing the goods to the US

You carry the burden of proof, and CBP can ask for these at any time.

### The practical hurdle
The trader must reveal its **purchase price, and therefore its margin**. Many refuse. Workarounds: disclose only to your broker or customs counsel under a confidentiality agreement, or make it a condition of your supply contract.

### China-specific points
- First sale is still available for Chinese goods, but CBP looks hard at Hong Kong intermediaries. Payments routed through third parties or invoices that look reconstructed are red flags.
- Origin stays China; Hong Kong routing doesn't change which tariffs apply.
- The lower value carries through to all ad valorem duties, including Section 301.

### Next steps
1. Ask the trader whether it will provide factory invoices and payment proof.
2. Have customs counsel or a broker review a sample transaction.
3. Apply it to new entries once documentation is solid, and ask whether recent unliquidated entries can be corrected.
<<<END ANSWER B>>>

### Checks

1. Explains the first sale rule: customs value can be based on the factory-to-middleman price if that sale is a bona fide sale for export to the US, at arm's length, with the goods clearly destined for the US at that time.
2. Notes the documentation needed (factory invoice, transfer of title and risk, proof of payment) and that the middleman must cooperate, which they may resist because it exposes their margin.
3. Notes that first sale lowers ad valorem duties, including Section 301 duties, but does not change country of origin.
4. Recommends working with a customs broker or attorney and declaring first sale correctly on entry.

## Item q04

### User message

Under the EU MDR, what risk class would clinical decision support software that suggests diagnoses to doctors fall into?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
**Short answer: at least Class IIa, and often Class IIb or III**, under **Rule 11** of Annex VIII of the EU MDR (2017/745).

### Step 1: Confirm it's a medical device
Software that suggests diagnoses for individual patients has a medical purpose under Article 2(1), so it qualifies as medical device software (MDSW). Unlike the US, the EU has **no carve-out** just because a clinician reviews the output; decision support is still a device. (Software that only stores, retrieves, or displays data without interpreting it may not qualify; see MDCG 2019-11.)

### Step 2: Apply Rule 11
Software intended to provide information used to take decisions with diagnostic or therapeutic purposes is:

| Class | When |
|---|---|
| **IIa** | Default |
| **IIb** | Decisions could cause a **serious deterioration** of health or a surgical intervention |
| **III** | Decisions could cause **death or an irreversible deterioration** of health |

The class depends on the **worst plausible consequence of a wrong or missed suggestion**, not on how rarely errors occur or whether a doctor is "in the loop."

### Illustrative examples
- Differential diagnoses for common, non-serious skin conditions → likely **IIa**
- Flagging possible fractures or pneumonia, where a miss could seriously harm the patient → likely **IIb**
- Suggesting stroke, sepsis, myocardial infarction, or cancer diagnoses, where a miss could be fatal or irreversible → potentially **III**

MDCG 2019-11 (revised 2025) maps Rule 11 onto the IMDRF framework (significance of the information × seriousness of the healthcare situation), which helps you justify the class. If the software interprets lab or other in vitro diagnostic data, check whether the **IVDR** applies instead.

### Why the class matters
- **Class IIa and above require a Notified Body**; no self-certification. Expect review of your QMS (ISO 13485) and technical documentation, typically under Annex IX.
- **Higher classes mean more scrutiny:** more extensive technical documentation review, stronger clinical evidence expectations, and more frequent PSURs (annually for IIb/III).
- **Clinical evaluation** (Article 61; MDCG 2020-1 for software) must show valid clinical association, technical performance, and clinical performance.
- **EU AI Act:** If the software uses AI/ML and needs Notified Body assessment, it's a **high-risk AI system** (Article 6(1)), adding obligations such as data governance, transparency, human oversight, and logging. These are currently scheduled to apply from August 2027, but watch for timeline changes under the EU's digital simplification proposals.

### Practical tip
Draft your **intended purpose** carefully (target conditions, users, care setting, and how outputs are used), since it drives the classification. Document your Rule 11 rationale and discuss it with a Notified Body early, as their capacity can be a bottleneck.
<<<END ANSWER A>>>

## Item q05

### User message

Can we say we're "the fastest CRM on the market" in our ads?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
**Short answer: only if you can prove it. "Fastest" is harder to prove than it sounds.**

"The fastest CRM on the market" is an objective superiority claim that can be measured. That puts it outside harmless puffery like "the CRM you'll love." In the US, the FTC requires you to have evidence for the claim *before* the ad runs. Competitors can also challenge you under the Lanham Act or through the National Advertising Division (NAD). The UK (ASA/CAP Code), the EU, Australia, and other markets have similar rules for comparative claims, and ad platforms may reject superlatives you can't support.

**What you'd need to back it up:**

1. **A clear definition of "fast."** It could mean page load time, search speed, setup time, or time to log a deal. Buyers may read it any of these ways, and you may have to support every reasonable reading.
2. **Testing against the whole market,** or at least every major competitor (Salesforce, HubSpot, Zoho, Pipedrive, Microsoft Dynamics, etc.). "On the market" means everyone.
3. **A fair, rigorous method.** That means comparable plans, configurations, and data volumes, tests that can be repeated, and ideally an independent third party running or checking them.
4. **Current results.** Competitors ship updates all the time, so last year's benchmark may no longer hold. Retest regularly.
5. **Documentation** you can hand over quickly if someone challenges the claim.

**Safer alternatives that still sell speed:**

- **Qualify it:** "Loads records 2x faster than [Competitor] in independent tests*", with a footnote explaining the method.
- **Make it specific and provable:** "Log a call in under 5 seconds."
- **Use customer evidence:** time-saved figures from a real customer survey.
- **Use subjective language:** "Built for speed" or "A CRM that keeps up with you" are generally treated as puffery.
- **Cite third-party recognition,** such as review-site badges, used within their terms.

**What happens if you get it wrong:** competitor challenges, forced ad withdrawals, legal costs, rejected ads, and lost trust. CRM buyers usually run trials, so they can check speed for themselves.

**My recommendation:** Don't run the line as written unless you have recent, rigorous benchmarks showing you beat every major competitor on a clearly defined speed measure. A specific, provable speed claim is safer, and it usually persuades buyers better. Have legal counsel review any comparative or superlative claim before launch.

*This isn't legal advice. Requirements vary by country and channel.*
<<<END ANSWER A>>>

## Item q06

### User message

My ACoS on Amazon is 30%. Is that good?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
**It depends on your margins and goals.** A 30% ACoS can be great, break-even, or losing money.

### Start with break-even ACoS
Break-even ACoS = your profit margin before ad spend.

Example: you sell at $40, and after product cost, referral fee, FBA fee, and shipping you keep $14. $14 ÷ $40 = **35% break-even ACoS**.
- If your break-even is 35%, a 30% ACoS is profitable on ad-driven sales.
- If your pre-ad margin is 25%, a 30% ACoS loses money on each ad sale.

### What's typical
Average ACoS on Amazon often lands around 25–35%, varying by category. Competitive categories (supplements, beauty, phone accessories) often run higher; quiet niches can sit in the teens. So 30% is roughly average, neither alarming nor exceptional on its own.

### Your goal matters
- **Launch or ranking push:** 40–60%+ can be acceptable short term if it builds sales velocity, reviews, and organic rank.

**Short version:** The CRA (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) requires manufacturers of "products with digital elements" to draw up and maintain a software bill of materials (SBOM) as part of their technical documentation. That obligation applies from **11 December 2027**.

### What it requires

- **Legal basis:** Annex I, Part II, point 1. Manufacturers must "identify and document vulnerabilities and components contained in products with digital elements, including by drawing up a software bill of materials in a commonly used and machine-readable format covering at the very least the top-level dependencies of the products."
- **Format:** In practice, SPDX or CycloneDX. The Commission may specify the format and minimum elements through implementing acts (Art. 13(24)), so details could tighten. Germany's BSI TR-03183-2 is a good preview of what regulators are likely to expect.
- **Depth:** Top-level (direct) dependencies are the legal floor. Full transitive SBOMs are far more useful for actual vulnerability management.
- **Confidentiality:** The SBOM is part of the technical documentation (Annex VII). You must provide it to market surveillance authorities on reasoned request, but you are not required to publish it; sharing it with users is optional.
- **Upkeep:** It underpins vulnerability handling for the whole support period (normally at least five years), so regenerate it with every release. Technical documentation must be kept for 10 years after placing on the market or for the support period, whichever is longer.

### Key dates

| Date | Milestone |
|---|---|
| 10 Dec 2024 | CRA entered into force |
| 11 Jun 2026 | Rules on conformity assessment bodies apply |
| 11 Sep 2026 | Reporting of actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents (24-hour early warning, 72-hour notification) via ENISA's single reporting platform; also covers products already on the market |
| **11 Dec 2027** | All remaining obligations, including essential requirements and the SBOM |

Products placed on the market before 11 December 2027 only fall under the full requirements if substantially modified afterwards; the reporting duties apply regardless.

### Scope and penalties

The CRA covers hardware and software made available commercially in the EU; importers and distributors have verification duties. Non-commercial open source is excluded, and "open-source software stewards" get a lighter regime. Breaching the essential requirements can cost up to €15 million or 2.5% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

### Practical advice

Automate SBOM generation in CI (e.g., Syft, cdxgen, or build-tool plugins), store one per release, and connect it to continuous vulnerability monitoring. You need most of that pipeline already to meet the reporting duties in force since September 2026.

*This is a general summary, not legal advice; check the regulation text and any implementing acts or harmonised standards relevant to your product category.*
