## Item q03

### User message

My friend says the LHC disproved string theory because it didn't find supersymmetry. Is that right?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
**Not really.** Your friend is pointing at a real result but drawing the wrong conclusion.

**What is true.** The LHC has searched hard for supersymmetric partners of the known particles and has not found them. ATLAS and CMS have pushed lower limits on many simplified supersymmetry models into the multi-TeV range. Many theorists, including some who work on string theory, were disappointed by this.

**What it does not show.** The null results constrain particular low-energy supersymmetry models, where the partners sit near the TeV scale. They do not rule out string theory as a framework, for three reasons:

1. Supersymmetry is not a TeV-scale prediction of string theory. Spacetime supersymmetry shows up in many controlled constructions, but the scale at which it is broken is a model choice, and it can sit far above LHC energies.
2. Non-supersymmetric string theories exist. The SO(16)xSO(16) heterotic string has no spacetime supersymmetry and is still free of tachyons.
3. Ruling out one model is not the same as ruling out the framework that contains it.

**Status in plain words.**

- 'String theory predicts superpartners at LHC energies' is a myth. The theory never promised that.
- 'The LHC has not found superpartners at accessible energies' is an established experimental fact, as of the latest results I know.
- 'String theory is wrong' is not supported by anything the LHC has shown. Equally, nothing has confirmed it. No strings, no extra dimensions and no superpartners have been directly detected.

**Where your friend has a fair point.** Before the LHC ran, many physicists expected supersymmetry near the TeV scale, partly for naturalness reasons and partly because it would have been an early clue about stringy physics. Many now think the naturalness argument has weakened, though this is contested. So the null result did remove some reasons to expect stringy physics soon. That disappointment is real. It is not the same as killing the theory.

**What would change the picture.** Discovering superpartners at the LHC or its high-luminosity upgrade would be important evidence about supersymmetry in nature. Even then, it would be a result about particular models, not a direct confirmation of strings.

**Sources.** The public ATLAS and CMS supersymmetry search summaries and the Particle Data Group review on supersymmetry are good places to start. These limits are updated often, so check arXiv hep-th and INSPIRE-HEP for the latest numbers before relying on any specific figure.
<<<END ANSWER A>>>

### Answer B

<<<BEGIN ANSWER B>>>
No, that doesn't follow. The LHC's failure to find supersymmetry (SUSY) is a real constraint, but it doesn't disprove string theory, for a few reasons:

- **String theory doesn't fix the SUSY-breaking scale.** Superstring theories need supersymmetry at the fundamental level, but the energy at which it gets broken is not predicted precisely. It could sit near the TeV scale the LHC probes, or far higher, where the LHC would never see superpartners.
- **The LHC ruled out specific models, not the whole idea.** Simple versions of SUSY, such as minimal supergravity, have been squeezed hard by LHC data. That's a statement about those particular models.
- **Missing particles don't refute a framework.** String theory is a large family of approaches rather than a single prediction. Its core claims, like a consistent quantum theory that includes gravity, don't depend on finding superpartners at the LHC.

The serious criticism is different. String theory has been very hard to test directly, and its flexibility (the so-called landscape of many possible vacuum states) makes sharp predictions difficult. That's a real debate about testability, but it isn't the same as the LHC disproving it.

So your friend has the experimental result roughly right but the conclusion wrong. String theory is unconfirmed and not directly testable with current experiments, but it hasn't been ruled out.

## Item q04

### User message

My ACoS on Amazon is 30%. Is that good?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
Not by itself. ACoS (advertising cost of sale) is ad spend divided by ad-attributed sales, so a 30% ACoS means you spend $0.30 on ads for every $1.00 of ad sales. Whether that is good depends on your margin.

**The real test is your break-even ACoS.** Break-even is roughly your profit margin before advertising. Take your selling price and subtract COGS, the Amazon referral fee, FBA fees and other per-unit costs. Divide what is left by the price. That percentage is the highest ACoS you can run without losing money on ad-driven sales.

Example: a $25 product with $6 COGS, a $3.75 referral fee (15%) and a $4 FBA fee leaves $11.25 per unit before ads. That is 45% of the price, so a 30% ACoS costs $7.50 per sale and leaves about $3.75 of profit per unit. Your numbers will differ, but the method is the same.

**How to read the result:**
- Above break-even: each ad-driven sale loses money, even if sales grow.
- Near break-even: ads mostly build rank and reviews. This can be acceptable short term.
- Well below break-even: you have room to bid more and capture more sales.

**What ACoS does not tell you:**
- Whether the account is profitable. Use TACoS (total ad spend divided by total sales, including organic) for that.
- Whether each keyword is worth it. Branded terms usually run a low ACoS, while broad or competitor terms often run higher. Judge each campaign against its own break-even.
- Long-term value. Some ad sales lift organic rank and reviews later.

**Next step:** Calculate your break-even ACoS, then set a target a few points below it. Pause or adjust keywords that stay above that line after a few weeks of data.
<<<END ANSWER A>>>

### Answer B

<<<BEGIN ANSWER B>>>
Short answer: 30% can be great or ruinous, and the number alone can't tell you which. What decides it is break-even ACoS, the point where ad spend consumes everything the product earns before ads.

**Step 1: Contribution margin before ads.** Take the selling price and subtract every per-unit cost that doesn't depend on ads:
- Amazon referral fee
- Fulfillment fee (FBA or merchant)
- Storage and aged-inventory charges, spread per unit
- Returns, net of what you recover
- Landed cost (product, freight, duty, inbound)
- Packaging and prep

Break-even ACoS = contribution margin before ads / selling price.

With made-up numbers: a $30 item with $10.80 of contribution before ads breaks even at 36% ACoS. At 30%, each ad-attributed sale keeps about $1.80 after its $9.00 of ad cost. That's positive, but only if those sales are truly incremental.

**Step 2: Check what the 30% is hiding.** ACoS counts only ad-attributed sales. It ignores organic sales the ads displaced and any organic lift they created. Read it next to:
- TACoS (total ad spend / total sales), which shows whether ads are a shrinking or growing share of revenue
- Organic sales trend for the same ASINs
- ACoS by campaign, since one campaign at 60% can hide an exact-match campaign at 12%

**Step 3: Test branded spend.** If a large share of the 30% comes from your own brand terms, you may be paying for shoppers who would have bought anyway. Pause branded campaigns for a defined window in one region or time period and compare total sales, not attributed sales.

**What I need before I call it:**
1. Your selling price and contribution per unit before ads, with each Amazon fee taken from Seller Central (Revenue Calculator or fee reports) and dated.
2. Ad spend, ad-attributed sales, and total sales for the same period.
3. The 30% split by campaign.

Until then, 30% is a number, not a verdict. Send those three and I'll give you a break-even, a target ACoS, and a bid rule.
<<<END ANSWER B>>>

2. Shows an example (e.g. with a 35% pre-ad margin, 30% ACoS is thinly profitable; with 25%, ad-driven sales lose money).

## Item q07

### User message

Does AdS/CFT prove that our universe is a hologram?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
**No.** AdS/CFT is a precise duality for a specific class of spacetimes, and our universe does not obviously belong to that class.

**What AdS/CFT says.** Maldacena's 1997 conjecture (arXiv hep-th/9711200) proposes that quantum gravity in anti-de Sitter space, a spacetime with negative cosmological constant, is equivalent to a conformal field theory living on the boundary of that space. The best-known case relates type IIB string theory on AdS5 x S5 to four-dimensional N=4 super Yang-Mills theory. This is a heavily checked conjecture with a precise dictionary and an enormous number of nontrivial tests. It has no general proof.

**Why it does not cover our universe.** The accelerating expansion is best described by a positive cosmological constant or something similar, so our universe looks more like de Sitter space than anti-de Sitter space. AdS/CFT does not apply to it directly. Holography for de Sitter space is an active research program, but it is not an established result. So 'our universe is a hologram' is an extrapolation, and its status is open.

**Where the metaphor breaks.** A hologram encodes a 3D image on a 2D film that you can point to. In AdS/CFT the boundary is a conformal boundary at infinity in the geometry, and the relation is a duality between two descriptions of the same physics. It is not a literal encoding of a picture onto a surface, and the boundary is not a place where our universe sits.

**What is better supported.** Some holographic ideas stand on firmer ground. Black-hole entropy scales with horizon area (S = A/4G), and the Ryu-Takayanagi formula relates entanglement entropy in a CFT to minimal surfaces in the bulk (arXiv hep-th/0603001). Holography has also been useful as a tool. The ratio of shear viscosity to entropy density in some strongly coupled plasmas matches the holographic value 1/(4 pi), and measured values in the quark-gluon plasma come close to it. Is it true that in string theory every particle is a tiny vibrating string and different "notes" are different particles? I have a physics undergrad degree, so explain it properly.

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
**Partly, but the popular version gets the important part wrong.**

**What is right.** In perturbative string theory, the fundamental objects are one-dimensional strings whose dynamics come from the Polyakov action on a two-dimensional worldsheet:

S = -(1/(4 pi alpha')) * integral d^2 sigma sqrt(-gamma) gamma^{ab} d_a X^mu d_b X^nu eta_{mu nu}

Quantizing the string gives a tower of states labeled by oscillation level N. Schematically m^2 is proportional to N/alpha', so the excited levels are heavy, near the string scale. Different oscillation patterns really are different states.

**Where the picture breaks.** The particles we observe, such as electrons, quarks, photons and gravitons, are not the higher harmonics. They are light modes. The violin analogy works up to this point, then fails. On a violin the overtones are audible and the fundamental is just the lowest note. In string theory the higher modes sit near the string scale, far beyond any accelerator. The light states that play the role of electrons and quarks get their charges, families and interactions mostly from the geometry of the extra dimensions, the arrangement of D-branes and background fluxes.

So 'different notes are different particles' is correct about the spectrum and misleading about which particles we see.

**Status, claim by claim.**

- The Polyakov action and the worldsheet spectrum are established mathematics.
- The superstring needs ten spacetime dimensions, because the worldsheet conformal anomaly cancels only for D = 10. That is a consistency requirement, not a choice.
- The massless closed-string sector contains a graviton, a Kalb-Ramond field and a dilaton. Gauge fields arise from D-branes or from the internal structure of the compactification.
- Some constructions reproduce chiral matter close to the Standard Model. The full Standard Model, including Yukawa couplings, moduli stabilization and the cosmological constant, is not derived. This is open.
- A complete nonperturbative definition is known only in special cases, such as AdS/CFT and matrix models. Treat the general case as open.

**Short version.** The electron is not a higher note. It is a light mode, and its properties come from the shape of the space it lives in.

Does AdS/CFT prove that our universe is a hologram?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
No. AdS/CFT is a powerful result, but it doesn't show that our universe is a hologram.

What AdS/CFT says: it is a duality, a conjecture with strong evidence rather than a general theorem, between two descriptions of the same physics. One side is quantum gravity (string theory) in anti-de Sitter (AdS) space, a spacetime with negative cosmological constant in d+1 dimensions. The other side is an ordinary quantum field theory without gravity (a conformal field theory, CFT) living on the d-dimensional boundary of that space. The best-studied example relates type IIB string theory on AdS5 x S5 to N=4 super Yang-Mills theory in four dimensions.

The 'hologram' label comes from the fact that the boundary theory encodes everything about the bulk, the way a flat hologram encodes a 3D image. But that encoding is established for a specific class of spacetimes.

Why this doesn't carry over to our universe:

- **Our universe isn't AdS.** AdS has a negative cosmological constant. Observations indicate a small positive cosmological constant, with accelerating expansion, which makes our universe closer to de Sitter space. Holography for de Sitter is much less understood.
- **A duality is not a proof about the real world.** It shows two mathematical descriptions are equivalent within a particular theory. It doesn't establish that nature is described by either one.
- **The general holographic principle is still a conjecture.** The idea that information in a region is bounded by its boundary area is motivated by black hole thermodynamics and is widely studied, but it is not established for our cosmos.
- **Experiments haven't confirmed it.** Fermilab's Holometer searched for a predicted holographic 'noise' in laser interferometers and found no signal at the sensitivity it reached.

A more accurate statement is that AdS/CFT is a precise example of holography in idealized settings, and an important tool for studying quantum gravity. Whether our universe is holographic in a similar sense is an open question that AdS/CFT does not settle.

## Item q16

### User message

We quantized our vision model to int8 with TFLite and it's actually slower than float on some Android phones. Why?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
Int8 being slower than float on some phones is almost always a placement problem. Integer kernels are faster in principle, but only when every op in the graph runs on a unit with a fast integer path.

Check these in order:

1. **CPU fallback.** On the slow phones, the delegate probably rejected some quantized ops, so the graph split into several partitions. Each split adds copies between memory domains, and the fallback kernel may be slow. One unsupported op can cost more than the rest of the model.
2. **Different delegate per model.** The float model may run on the GPU delegate at FP16 while the int8 model lands on a CPU path or an NPU path that is weak on that SoC. Compare the same delegate on the same device.
3. **Extra quantize and dequantize nodes.** If conversion left these around ops that did not quantize cleanly, you pay for the conversions on every inference.
4. **Vendor differences.** An NPU path that is fast on one SoC can be missing or slow on another. Int8 is not a universal win across Android hardware.
5. **Measurement.** The float number may be a warm burst while the int8 number includes a cold compile, or the reverse.

To find out:

- Read the placement report on each slow device and count partitions. Every unexplained CPU partition is a suspect.
- Run LiteRT's benchmark_model with op profiling on the slowest supported phone, for both models, same delegate, after warmup.
- Find the op that dominates the int8 time. That is where the work is.

What I would do:

- Rewrite or replace the ops the accelerator rejects. Check that the full-integer conversion uses int8 inputs and outputs, so you are not converting at the boundaries.
- Choose the delegate per tier. If int8 loses on the low tier, ship float there and int8 only where the placement is clean.
- Re-run the accuracy gate on the faster variant against the float baseline, with a calibration set drawn from production inputs.

Report p50 and p95 on the slowest device, with first minute and steady state separated, plus the LiteRT version and delegate. Support for quantized ops changes between releases, so check the current LiteRT delegate documentation against the version you pinned.

Which phones are slower, which delegate are you using, and do you have the placement report for one of them? That will tell us which of these it is.

### Answer B

<<<BEGIN ANSWER B>>>
Quantized int8 models are usually faster on paper, but on real Android hardware the speedup depends on whether the device can run your int8 ops efficiently. Common causes:

**1. The int8 graph isn't fully accelerated.** If you use the NNAPI or GPU delegate, it only takes the ops it supports. Unsupported ops fall back to the CPU, and the graph splits into partitions with data copies between them. A float model is more likely to be fully delegated, so it can win overall. Check the delegate log for the partition count, or use TFLite's benchmark tool to see which ops run where.

**2. Quantize/dequantize boundaries.** If the model isn't fully integer (float inputs or outputs, or a few ops left in float), TFLite inserts Quantize and Dequantize ops at the boundaries. These add latency, especially with many small tensors.

**3. CPU kernel differences.** XNNPACK has highly tuned float32 NEON kernels. Its int8 kernels rely on CPU features such as dot-product (SDOT) and i8mm. On older mid-range SoCs without those instructions, int8 can be no faster than float32, and sometimes slower, because of requantization overhead.

**4. Small or memory-light models.** Quantization mainly saves memory bandwidth and speeds up the math. If the model is small, or latency is dominated by preprocessing, copies or framework overhead, the gain is hidden or reversed.

**5. Measurement noise.** Thermal throttling, first-run delegate compilation, battery-saver mode or different thread counts between runs can make one variant look slower.

**How to diagnose**

- Confirm the graph is full integer quantization with int8 input and output, not dynamic-range or hybrid.
- Run `benchmark_model` with `--use_nnapi=true`, `--use_gpu=true`, `--num_threads=4` and `--enable_op_profiling=true` on the affected devices.
- Compare float and int8 on the same device, with the same thread count, after warm-up.
- Check which ops are delegated and where partitions break.

**Likely fixes**

- Re-export with full integer quantization and int8 I/O, using per-channel weights where supported.
- Replace or rewrite ops the delegate doesn't support.
- Choose the backend per device: for example, GPU FP16 on some devices, NPU int8 on others, XNNPACK float elsewhere.
- Ship both variants and pick at runtime from a short on-device benchmark, or keep a device allowlist.

Start with the per-op profile on the slow device. In most cases you'll find a delegate fallback or a quantize/dequantize boundary.
<<<END ANSWER B>>>
