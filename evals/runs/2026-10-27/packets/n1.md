## Item q03

### User message

A supplier says their paraffin phase-change material stores 200 kJ/kg, so their 1,000 kg module stores 55 kWh, and that it charges at 50 kW. Should I believe these numbers?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
**Short answer:** the arithmetic is right, but both numbers are probably best-case. Ask for test data before you rely on them.

**Energy (55 kWh)**

200 kJ/kg × 1,000 kg = 200 MJ = 55.6 kWh, so the conversion holds. What matters is what the 200 kJ/kg covers:

- **PCM mass or module mass?** The container, tubes and fins are often 15–40% of a module's weight. If 1,000 kg is the whole module, you might have 600–850 kg of PCM, or ~33–47 kWh.
- **Latent only, or latent plus sensible?** Paraffins have latent heats of ~150–250 kJ/kg, so 200 is plausible. But datasheets often quote capacity over a wide band (e.g., 15 K around the melt point), which adds sensible heat. Over a narrower operating band you'll get less.
- **Usable vs. theoretical.** Some PCM may not fully melt or freeze within your cycle time. Real modules often deliver 70–90% of nameplate.
- **Melting range.** Commercial paraffins melt over several degrees, so make sure that range sits comfortably inside your charge and discharge temperatures.

**Power (50 kW)**

That implies a full charge in ~1.1 hours. That's possible, but paraffin conducts heat poorly (~0.2 W/m·K), so power depends entirely on the heat-exchanger design. Ask:

- **Peak or average?** Power is highest at first and falls as the melt front moves away from the tubes.
- **Under what conditions?** You need the inlet temperature, flow rate and driving ΔT. 50 kW at a 30 K ΔT doesn't mean 50 kW at 5 K.
- **Discharge power.** It's usually lower than charge power, because solidified PCM coats the tubes and insulates them. It's often the number that matters most.

**Request from the supplier**

1. DSC (enthalpy vs. temperature) data and the PCM mass in the module.
2. Measured charge/discharge curves (power and outlet temperature vs. time) at stated conditions, ideally from an independent lab.
3. Cycling-stability data and any certification (e.g., RAL-GZ 896).
4. Heat-loss figures and safety details. Paraffin is flammable and expands ~10–15% on melting.

**Bottom line:** treat 55 kWh as a theoretical ceiling, and plan on roughly 30–45 kWh usable until test data shows otherwise. Treat 50 kW as a likely peak until you see a measured power curve at *your* operating conditions.
<<<END ANSWER A>>>

## Item q04

### User message

Our factory needs steam at 400 °C around the clock. We plan to charge storage from solar during the day. Should we store the heat in molten salt or in concrete?

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
**Short answer:** for steam at a steady 400 °C around the clock, two-tank molten salt is usually the better fit, as long as your solar source can reach ~550 °C. Concrete struggles because its output temperature falls as it discharges. But first check how you'll charge the storage.

**Why molten salt**

- **Constant output temperature.** The hot tank stays at full temperature (up to ~565 °C for solar salt) until it's empty, so the steam doesn't cool overnight.
- **Proven.** Many CSP plants run multi-GWh salt storage for 24-hour operation.
- **Downsides:** salt freezes around 220–240 °C, so you need heat tracing and freeze protection. You also need hot-salt pumps and valves and corrosion management, and it only pays off above a fairly large size.

**Why concrete is harder here**

- **Sliding temperature.** Outlet temperature drops during discharge, so delivering 400 °C at dawn means charging to ~500 °C+. That's near concrete's practical limit, where cracking under thermal cycling becomes a concern.
- **Low conductivity** (~1–2 W/m·K). You need lots of embedded steel tubing, and the tubing dominates the cost.
- **Upsides:** cheap media, no freezing, modular, low maintenance. It's well suited to lower temperatures or preheating duty.

**Check these first**

1. **Your solar source.**
   - Thermal-oil troughs top out near 390 °C, which isn't enough.
   - A tower or salt troughs pair naturally with salt storage.
   - **If you're charging from PV with electric heaters**, look at electrically heated brick or ceramic "heat batteries" (600–1,000 °C). They deliver 400 °C steam easily.
2. **Steam pressure.** Most of the energy goes into evaporation, often well below 400 °C. A hybrid can cut cost: sensible storage for preheat and superheat, plus a phase-change stage for evaporation.
3. **Sizing.** With ~8 sun hours, the solar field must be ~3× the load, and storage must cover ~16 h. For example, a 5 MW load needs ~80 MWh, roughly 700 t of salt over a 290–565 °C swing.
4. **Backup** for cloudy stretches (gas or electric boiler).

**Bottom line:** if you're on high-temperature solar thermal, choose molten salt. Choose concrete only with a top-up superheater or a hybrid design. If you're on PV, seriously consider high-temperature solid "heat batteries". Base the final decision on a feasibility study using your hourly steam profile.
<<<END ANSWER A>>>

### Answer A

<<<BEGIN ANSWER A>>>
**Short answer:** I can't tell you which one applies. That is a legal question for your counsel. What I can tell you is that the two versions say different things, and nobody should sign until they say the same thing.

**The discrepancy**

| | English | Arabic |
|---|---|---|
| Text | liquidated damages are capped at 10% of the contract value | غرامة التأخير بحد أقصى ١٥٪ من قيمة العقد |
| Cap | 10% | ١٥٪ = **15%** |
| What is capped | liquidated damages | غرامة التأخير = delay penalty |

The versions differ in two ways:

1. **The number.** The English caps it at 10%. The Arabic says ١٥٪, which is 15% (10% would be ١٠٪). On an illustrative SAR 10 million contract, that is a cap of SAR 1.5 million against SAR 1 million.
2. **The scope.** "Liquidated damages" can cover more than delay, such as performance shortfalls. غرامة التأخير covers delay only. The usual Arabic for LDs is التعويض الاتفاقي or الشرط الجزائي. So the two clauses may not even cap the same thing.

- **Most countries, including Europe and China, generally require "absolute novelty."** Anything made public before your filing date can permanently block a patent there. Narrow exceptions exist, but they rarely cover an ordinary conference talk.

**What counts as disclosure**

- The talk, slides, and poster
- Abstracts and proceedings — often published online *before* the event (check whether yours already is)
- Recordings, livestreams, and shared slide decks
- What you say in Q&A or hallway conversations

**What to do now**

1. **Contact a patent attorney or your tech-transfer office this week.** A month is tight but workable.

5. **Check your obligations.** University, employer, grant, or partner agreements often require invention disclosures or approval before publication.

- Evidence of no through-porosity (e.g., a gas leak test).

**2. Test setup**
- Cell schematic or photo, sealing method, and the **exposed active area** used in the calculation.
- Driving force: applied current or voltage and electrodes, or concentration gradient.
- Composition, volume, pH, temperature and stirring/flow of both feed and receiving solutions.

**3. Flux calculation**
- The equation, units (e.g., mol m⁻² h⁻¹) and the time window used.
- Raw concentration–time data for both chambers, showing the linear region.
- For electrically driven tests: current, cell voltage, **Faradaic efficiency** for Li⁺, and energy per mol or kg Li. Flux without efficiency is hard to interpret.

**4. Analytical rigor**
- ICP-OES/MS method, calibration range, detection limits, matrix-matched standards, blanks and dilution factors.

**5. Controls against artifacts**
- Measure Na, K, Mg and Ca on the receiving side. LLTO should exclude them, so their presence flags cracks, seal leaks or grain-boundary paths. Report the resulting selectivities.
- Run a zero-current (diffusion-only) control.
- **Mass balance:** Li lost from the feed vs. gained on the receiving side. Gaps may indicate Li uptake, Li⁺/H⁺ exchange in LLTO, or sampling losses.
- Track pH on both sides, since proton exchange and water splitting can affect results.

**6. Reproducibility**
- At least three independently fabricated membranes, mean ± SD, and a clear statement of whether reported values are averages or best results.
- Raw data and complete methods in the SI.

**7. Stability**
- Flux and efficiency over extended operation or repeated cycles.
- Post-test XRD, SEM and XPS to check for cracking, proton exchange, Ti⁴⁺ reduction or surface deposits.

**8. Fair benchmarking**
- A comparison table in the same units that notes thickness, driving force and Li feed concentration, since these strongly affect flux.

Also state the Li feed concentration prominently. If you used high-Li synthetic solutions, add at least one test at realistic brine or seawater levels, where flux and efficiency can drop sharply.

The points reviewers most often challenge are active area, leakage and reproducibility. Address those explicitly and your number will be far more convincing.
<<<END ANSWER B>>>

2. Asks for flux with units (e.g. mol m⁻² h⁻¹), membrane area, thickness, and test duration.
